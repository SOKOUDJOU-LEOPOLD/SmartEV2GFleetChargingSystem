Literature Survey: Risk-Aware Deep Reinforcement Learning for EV Charging Under Unexpected Departures
Yeshas Krishna and Leopold Sokoudjou Gatsing
1. Summary of Current Project Plans
Our core plans are unchanged from the proposal. Refinements to our experimental design that resulted from this survey are marked in bold in Section 1.1. Our project asks whether a standard deep reinforcement learning agent can learn to hedge against unexpected EV departures that it is never warned about. Using the EV2Gym simulation environment, we will train a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent, implemented with Stable-Baselines3, to manage charging and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) discharging across a 20-port charging site while accounting for electricity prices, transformer limits, and drivers’ charge requirements.
Existing smart-charging setups, including EV2Gym’s own V2G benchmark, reveal each EV’s departure time to the agent and treat it as accurate. We introduce a custom UnexpectedDepartureWrapper that, for a chosen fraction of EVs, secretly sets an earlier true departure time. The agent continues to observe only the originally reported time until the vehicle is gone, and is penalized for any charge shortfall only after the departure occurs. Our research question is: Can a model-free RL agent optimize V2G fleet scheduling under partial observability, implicitly managing the risk of unannounced early departures, and what economic or grid-performance trade-offs emerge from this robust policy?
Our core experiment compares two PPO agents that are identical except that one experiences early departures during training. Both are tested in an environment with unexpected departures, alongside a Charge-As-Fast-As-Possible (AFAP) baseline. We will vary the early-departure probability (p_early ∈ {0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20}) and how early vehicles leave. We will evaluate charging cost and V2G profit, the frequency and size of charge shortfalls at departure, energy sold to the grid, and transformer violations.
As a stretch goal, we will compare feedforward PPO against Recurrent PPO with an LSTM policy, using early-departure patterns estimated from Caltech’s ACN-Data (requested versus actual departure times), to test whether memory helps the agent recognize higher-risk charging situations. The way we generate departure risk for this comparison has changed; see the fifth refinement in Section 1.1.
1.1 Changes Since the Proposal
Based on this literature review, we have made five refinements to how we will run and evaluate our experiments. Our research question and core experiment are unchanged.
First, because prior work shows that the penalty weight largely determines where an agent lands on the trade-off between cost and undercharging [4], [7], we will train both agents at several values of λ_departure and compare them by their profit-versus-shortfall trade-off curves rather than by total reward at a single weight. We will choose the range of λ_departure so that the baseline agent satisfies most drivers when departures are accurate.
Second, we will add an oracle PPO agent that observes each EV’s true departure time, giving an upper bound on performance and showing how much of the gap a hedging agent can close [6].
Third, we will train each configuration with multiple random seeds and report the variation across runs.
Fourth, we will evaluate every trained agent both with and without early departures in a full train/test grid [7], and we will measure hedging directly by tracking each EV’s charge margin and V2G discharge as its reported departure approaches.
Fifth, we have revised the design of our stretch goal. EV2Gym’s observation already includes the current timestep, so if early-departure risk depends only on time of day, a feedforward policy can in principle learn that pattern without memory, and the comparison would not isolate the value of memory. We will therefore test two conditions. In the first, the early-departure probability varies with time of day, p_early(t), as originally proposed; this serves as a control in which memory should offer little advantage. In the second, each simulated day draws a hidden risk level (for example, a low- or high-early-departure day) that is never shown to the agent but can be inferred from history, because EVs that have already left before their reported time appear in past observations but not in the current one. Memory is expected to help mainly in this second condition. Where possible, we will use ACN-Data to set realistic early-departure probabilities and magnitudes, including how much the early-departure rate varies from day to day.
2. Related Work
We group the related work into four themes: simulation environments and data (Section 2.1), deep reinforcement learning for EV charging (Section 2.2), departure uncertainty (Section 2.3), and the algorithmic foundations of our method (Section 2.4).
2.1 Simulation Environments and Data
These works define the environment we build on and provide the empirical evidence that reported departure times are unreliable.
EV2Gym [1]. Orfanoudakis et al. introduce EV2Gym, an open-source, Gymnasium-compatible simulator for EV smart charging and V2G research. As their comparison shows, earlier RL-ready simulators either lack V2G support (ACN-Sim, SustainGym) or rely on identical EVs with randomized behavior (Chargym). EV2Gym populates its simulations with real data. EV arrivals, departure times, and arrival SoC are sampled from ElaadNL charging distributions conditioned on time of day, prices come from Dutch day-ahead markets, and each transformer carries inflexible load and solar generation. In the V2G profit-maximization benchmark, each EV reports its departure time and desired energy on arrival, and that reported time is always accurate. The RL agent observes the current timestep, per-charger SoC and time to departure, and price and load forecasts. It outputs a continuous action in [−1, 1] per charger and is rewarded for profit minus penalties for transformer overload and user dissatisfaction. Benchmarking Stable-Baselines3 algorithms on 10 bidirectional chargers, the authors find that PPO earns about €24 in profit but reaches only 46% user satisfaction, compared with 100% for the optimal solution.
We adopt EV2Gym’s environment, observation space, and AFAP baseline unchanged. Our key difference is that our wrapper separates the reported departure time from the true one, so the agent can no longer trust what it observes. We also replace the exponential satisfaction penalty with a squared SoC-shortfall penalty applied to every departing EV, so that scheduled and early departures are penalized identically. Finally, because PPO already undercharges drivers even with perfect departure information, undercharging rates alone cannot show the effect of hidden departures. Our experiment therefore compares two PPO agents that differ only in whether they experienced early departures during training.
ACN-Data [2]. Lee, Li, and Low release ACN-Data, an open dataset of more than 30,000 workplace charging sessions from two California sites: Caltech, with 54 chargers in a semi-public garage, and JPL, with 52 chargers restricted to employees. Each session records when the EV plugged in, when it finished charging, when it actually unplugged, and the energy delivered. For sessions where drivers used the mobile app, it also records their estimated departure time and requested energy. The authors model driver behavior with Gaussian mixture models fit over arrival time, session duration, and energy, at both the population and the individual-driver level, and predict each session’s duration from its arrival time. Their key finding is that driver-entered departure estimates are the least reliable predictor available. At JPL, user estimates of session duration had a symmetric mean absolute percentage error of 18.6%, compared with 12.3% for an individual-driver model. At Caltech, the gap was 25.8% versus 15.9%. The authors attribute this partly to drivers having little incentive to report accurately.
ACN-Data provides the empirical motivation for our project: the departure times that smart-charging controllers rely on are often wrong in practice. It is also the dataset we plan to use for our stretch goal, where the gap between requested and actual departure times will set realistic early-departure probabilities and magnitudes. Our approach differs in how it responds to unreliable departure information. Lee et al. replace the user’s estimate with a better forecast; our core experiment keeps the unreliable estimate in the agent’s observation and asks whether the policy itself learns to hedge against it.
2.2 Deep Reinforcement Learning for EV Charging
These works established the model-free formulation of EV charging and illustrate the main ways prior work handles the requirement to charge a vehicle before it leaves.
Wan et al. [3]. Wan, Li, He, and Prokhorov established the model-free formulation that much of the later EV charging RL work builds on. They model the charging of a single EV under real-time electricity pricing as a Markov decision process with unknown transition probabilities, where randomness comes from electricity prices and the driver’s commuting behavior, including arrival and departure times. Instead of forecasting these quantities and then optimizing, as model-based approaches do, the agent learns directly from experience. Their architecture has two parts. A representation network uses an LSTM to extract features from the recent history of electricity prices, and a Q-network maps those features, together with the battery’s remaining energy, to the values of a discrete set of charging and discharging actions. The reward is the negative charging cost, with penalties when the EV leaves without enough charge. Experiments on real price data show the learned policy reduces charging cost relative to baseline strategies.
Wan et al. show that a model-free agent can absorb uncertainty in driver behavior purely through the rewards it experiences, which is the same mechanism our project relies on. Our work differs in three ways. First, we control a 20-port site sharing a transformer rather than a single EV, so the agent must trade off between vehicles and respect a shared power limit. Second, we use continuous per-charger actions with PPO rather than discrete actions with DQN. Most importantly, Wan et al.’s agent is never given a departure time at all, so the question of trusting an inaccurate one never arises. In our setting, the agent receives a departure time that is usually correct, and the challenge is learning how much to rely on it.
Chen and Shi [4]. Chen and Shi address a single EV that charges and discharges under time-varying prices, with uncertain arrival time, departure time, and arrival energy. Rather than adding a penalty to the reward for leaving undercharged, they formulate the problem as a constrained Markov decision process. The reward is the negative charging cost, and a separate cost signal measures the shortfall from the target charge at departure plus any drop below a minimum charge floor. The agent observes the current state of charge and the past 24 hours of prices, and outputs a continuous charging or discharging power. To solve the constrained problem, they combine soft actor-critic (SAC) with an augmented Lagrangian method. Separate critic networks estimate the expected reward and the expected constraint cost, the actor maximizes the resulting Lagrangian, and the constraint’s multiplier is updated automatically during training instead of being hand-tuned. On 175 test days of German price data, their method achieved zero average constraint violation at an average cost of €0.113 per day, close to the €0.068 of an MPC with perfect information. In contrast, standard SAC with a hand-picked penalty weight either violated the constraint (with a small weight) or met it at roughly six times the cost, €0.697 per day (with a large weight).
This result is directly relevant to our design, because it shows that the penalty weight, not just the algorithm, determines where an agent lands on the trade-off between cost and undercharging. We deliberately keep a fixed penalty weight λ_departure so that our two PPO agents are trained on exactly the same reward and differ only in their environment. Given Chen and Shi’s findings, we will test more than one value of λ_departure so that any hedging behavior we observe is not an artifact of a single choice. Their setting also differs from ours: the agent is never shown a departure time, whereas ours receives one that is usually correct but sometimes wrong. Extending our experiment to a constrained formulation like theirs is a natural follow-up.
Sadeghianpourhamami et al. [5]. Sadeghianpourhamami, Deleu, and Develder move from controlling a single EV to jointly coordinating a whole group of charging stations for demand response. Their main contribution is an MDP formulation whose state representation does not grow with the number of stations. Rather than tracking each EV individually, they place connected EVs on a two-dimensional grid according to how much connection time each has left and how much charging time it still needs, and normalize the counts so the representation is independent of fleet size. The action specifies, for each group of EVs with the same flexibility (the slack between remaining connection time and remaining charging time), the fraction of those EVs to charge at full power during the next time slot. The cost to minimize is the square of the total power drawn, which flattens the aggregate load, plus a large penalty for every EV that can no longer finish charging before it leaves. Because historical charging data is available, they learn the policy offline with fitted Q-iteration, a batch RL algorithm that fits a Q-function to a fixed set of past transitions using function approximation. Using real-world charging sessions, they study how the amount of training data affects performance, compare the learned policy against an oracle with full knowledge of future arrivals, track performance across a full year, and show that a policy trained on one fleet size generalizes to larger groups of stations.
Like our project, this work coordinates many EVs under a shared constraint rather than optimizing one vehicle in isolation. However, its state depends directly on each EV’s remaining connection time, which assumes the reported departure time is correct. An EV that leaves early would sit in the wrong part of the grid until it disappears, and the policy would have no way of detecting the error. This is precisely the failure mode our wrapper is designed to expose. We also differ in scope and method: their setting has no V2G discharging, and they use offline batch RL with a fleet-level aggregate state, whereas we use online PPO with per-charger observations. We keep per-charger observations deliberately, because we want to inspect individual EVs to see whether the policy maintains an energy cushion.
2.3 Departure Uncertainty
These two works are the closest to ours: both train RL agents when departure times are uncertain, and they reach different conclusions about how the agent should respond.
Gabriele et al. [6]. Gabriele, Pavirani, Karimi Madahi, and Develder address EV charging when the departure time is not known to the controller, which makes this one of the most closely related works to our project. Their approach is to add a learned forecast of the remaining session duration to the RL agent’s state. A neural network predicts the total session length from the time of day, whether it is a weekend, the energy charged so far, and the energy still needed, and the agent uses this prediction in place of the true departure. Their key idea is to train the forecaster end-to-end with the controller rather than purely for accuracy. The forecaster’s loss blends a standard regression error with the soft actor-critic (SAC) policy loss, so the forecast is pushed toward whatever helps the agent make better charging decisions. The setting is a single EV with binary actions (charge at 6.5 kW or stay idle), a fixed daily price profile, and a reward of negative charging cost plus a squared penalty on unmet energy applied only when the EV leaves. On 120 real hospital-parking sessions, an agent with no departure information left 14.7% of demand unmet, a conventionally trained forecaster reduced this to 7.3%, and their decision-focused method reached 6.3% while fully charging about ten more EVs. Notably, the decision-focused forecaster learned to predict departures slightly earlier than they actually occurred, which led the agent to start charging sooner.
Our project shares two important design choices with this work: the undercharge penalty arrives only at the moment of departure, and the agent receives no advance warning. Their finding that a pessimistic estimate of departure time improves outcomes supports our hypothesis that the right adaptation is conservatism about time. However, the two approaches differ fundamentally. Gabriele et al. address the uncertainty by adding a learned departure forecast to the agent’s state; we leave the observation unchanged, keeping a reported departure time that is sometimes wrong, and ask whether the policy itself learns to hedge against it. Their setting also has no V2G, a single EV, and on/off charging, so the only way to hedge is to charge earlier. In our V2G fleet, the agent can also hedge by discharging less, which carries a direct cost in lost revenue. That is the trade-off our research question asks about. Finally, their result offers a prediction for our stretch goal: if a recurrent policy succeeds, it should behave as if it had learned a similarly pessimistic estimate of departure time.
Naghdizadegan Jahromi et al. [7]. Naghdizadegan Jahromi, Genov, Coosemans, and De Cauwer use PPO to control unidirectional EV charging at a 10-charger office parking facility that participates directly in the Belgian real-time imbalance market. Each charger runs the same decentralized policy, which observes the time of day, the EV’s expected departure time, its state of charge, and the current imbalance price and its change. It chooses among three actions: idle, half-rate, or full-rate charging. The reward combines a normalized charging cost with an energy-delivery term that rewards charging progress, scaled by urgency as departure approaches, and penalizes distance from the target charge at every step and at departure. The two weights are tuned with Optuna. To model departure uncertainty, the observed departure time is a forecast, and the actual departure differs from it by an error sampled from an empirical error distribution, so every EV may leave earlier or later than reported. The agent trained without departure uncertainty earned €507 over the eight-month test period with 92.4% energy delivery, and learned near-binary on/off control. The agent trained with uncertainty adopted a more conservative policy, using the half-rate action and buying energy at less favorable prices to secure a safety margin. This raised delivery to 94.2% but reduced revenue to €311. Cross-evaluation showed the no-uncertainty policy also achieved higher total reward in the uncertain environment (−593 versus −695), so the authors conclude that aggressive arbitrage outweighs conservative hedging. A sensitivity analysis showed that changing either reward weight by 20% shifted delivery between roughly 82% and 97% and swung the outcome from €1,631 in revenue to a €448 net cost.
This is the work most similar to ours in method, and its results inform our design in three ways. First, it shows that training under departure uncertainty does induce hedging behavior: the agent learned a safety margin without being told when departures would differ. Whether that hedging is judged worthwhile depended on the reward weights, which also determined total reward. We will therefore report shortfall metrics separately from reward and test more than one penalty weight. Second, the authors use cross-environment evaluation, testing each policy in the other’s environment, to separate what each policy learned from effects of the training process, and they attribute the uncertainty-trained policy’s lower reward to convergence to a fulfillment-heavy local solution. We adopt the same train/test grid and, because RL outcomes can vary between training runs, additionally repeat each configuration over multiple random seeds. Third, our setting differs in ways that may change the conclusion. Their departure errors are two-sided and affect every EV, whereas ours are one-sided early departures affecting a fraction of EVs, so a trusting policy faces only downside risk. Their penalty is dense, giving the agent continuous feedback, whereas ours arrives only at departure. Finally, they study charging-only control without a shared transformer limit; in our V2G fleet, hedging means forgoing discharge revenue while coordinating many vehicles under one power constraint.
2.4 Algorithmic Foundations and Partial Observability
These works underpin the learning algorithm we use and the reasoning behind our stretch goal.
Schulman et al. [8]. Schulman et al. introduce Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an on-policy actor-critic algorithm that keeps the stability of trust-region methods while being simple to implement. Instead of imposing a hard constraint on how far the policy can change, PPO optimizes a clipped surrogate objective: the ratio between the new and old action probabilities is clipped to the range [1 − ε, 1 + ε] inside the objective, which removes the incentive for a single update to move the policy far without strictly preventing it. This allows several epochs of minibatch optimization on each batch of experience.
We use PPO for both of our agents. Its clipped objective is well suited to our problem: unexpected departures produce rare, large penalties that create high-variance advantage estimates, and clipping discourages a single batch containing such an event from changing the policy too much. That same caution may cost profit, since PPO earned less than off-policy methods in the EV2Gym benchmark [1]. Because PPO has no built-in way to enforce charge requirements, any hedging our agent learns must come entirely from the reward signal.
Ni et al. [9]. Ni, Eysenbach, and Salakhutdinov revisit the common view that recurrent model-free RL, meaning a standard RL algorithm whose actor and critic use recurrent networks to summarize past observations, performs poorly on partially observable problems compared with specialized methods. They show that this reputation stems mostly from implementation choices. Using separate recurrent encoders for the actor and critic, feeding the previous action and reward as inputs, and carefully choosing the base algorithm and the length of history the network sees, their implementation matched or outperformed specialized methods on 18 of 21 tasks. These included occluded robot locomotion, meta-RL, and robust RL, where a hidden environment parameter must be inferred from experience.
This work supports our stretch goal of comparing feedforward PPO with Recurrent PPO. Our hidden-risk condition (Section 1.1) has the same structure as their robust-RL tasks: a parameter that is fixed within an episode and never observed directly, but that can be inferred from the history of observations, here from EVs that have already disappeared before their reported departure time. Their findings also tell us that history length is a key hyperparameter to tune rather than leave at a default. Their method differs in using off-policy algorithms (TD3 and SAC) rather than PPO, so a positive result with Recurrent PPO is not guaranteed.
Kaelbling et al. [10]. Kaelbling, Littman, and Cassandra provide the standard formulation of partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs). A POMDP extends an MDP with a set of observations and an observation function that gives the probability of each observation given the resulting state and action. Because the agent cannot see the true state, they show that the right basis for decisions is the belief state, a probability distribution over possible states that is updated with Bayes’ rule after each action and observation. This turns the POMDP into an MDP over beliefs, which they solve with value iteration, including their witness algorithm. They also show that exact solutions quickly become intractable as problems grow, which is why practical agents often act on recent observations or a learned summary of history instead.
This framework lets us state our problem precisely. Our wrapper makes each EV’s true departure time a hidden part of the state, while the observation carries only the reported time, so identical observations can lead to different outcomes. The framework also clarifies when memory should help. If early departures occur independently with a fixed probability, past observations provide little additional information about which EVs will leave early, and a feedforward policy can only respond by being uniformly more cautious. If departure risk varies with something already in the observation, such as the current time of day, a feedforward policy can learn that relationship without memory. A recurrent policy should offer an advantage mainly when the history reveals hidden conditions that the current observation does not, such as an unusually high rate of early departures on a given day. This is why our stretch goal tests both a time-of-day condition and a hidden daily risk level, rather than assuming that memory necessarily improves performance.
3. Summary and Positioning
Across this literature, fleet-level studies give the controller accurate departure times [1], [5], even though ACN-Data shows that reported departure times are frequently wrong in practice [2]. The two works that confront departure uncertainty directly [6], [7] study single-EV or charging-only settings and reach different conclusions: in one, a pessimistic departure estimate reduced unmet demand; in the other, training under uncertainty produced a safety margin that the chosen reward weights judged not worth its cost. Our project tests this question in a V2G fleet, where hedging means giving up discharge revenue under a shared transformer limit, and where the agent observes a specific departure time that is usually, but not always, correct. By comparing agents across several penalty weights, against an oracle, and over multiple random seeds, we aim to show whether, and at what cost, a standard PPO agent learns to protect drivers from departures it is never warned about.
